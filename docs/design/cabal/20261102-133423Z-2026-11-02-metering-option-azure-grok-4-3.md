## Response

1. Choose Option A. It delivers usable accuracy at low loads without the phase-shift and linearity problems of cheap CT/VT modules, and the isolation barrier is crossed only by a single, well-specified part rather than by two dubious transformers plus their wiring. For a one-off personal device the extra components are manageable if you pick a reinforced-rated isolator and maintain the 8 mm creepage you already have.

2. Use a second small isolated AC-DC module (another IRM-03-5 or equivalent) for the BL0942 and isolator primary side. A capacitive dropper is workable only if you add a series resistor (22–47 Ω, 2 W flame-proof), a proper X2 safety capacitor, a MOV directly across the incoming mains, and a fuse before everything; even then you still have the risk of the dropper capacitor failing short and putting hundreds of volts on the “low-voltage” rail. A second IRM eliminates all of those variables and keeps your supply topology consistent.

3. Use a digital isolator (ISO6721, ISOW7741, etc.) rather than optocouplers; at 4800 baud the isolator is simpler, lower power, and has better CMTI. The part itself must be rated reinforced isolation for 230 V mains—typically 5 kVrms or higher with a minimum 8 mm creepage package (wide SOIC-16 or larger). Check the datasheet for “reinforced,” VIOTM ≥ 5 kV, and pollution-degree-2 creepage/clearance numbers; anything less does not count as reinforced under IEC 62368/60664.

4. Neither the ZMPT101B nor the ZMCT103C is rated as a safety isolation barrier. They are low-cost functional-sensing transformers with basic or no guaranteed insulation distance, no certified creepage, and no hi-pot testing to reinforced levels. Relying on them to keep the SELV side safe is a misunderstanding of what they are.

5. Kelvin (four-terminal) connections are mandatory; any shared copper between the sense traces and the 10 A path adds milliohms of error that you cannot calibrate out. Thermal EMF is irrelevant on AC but the shunt will still run warm at 10 A continuous—keep it away from the ESP32 and USB connector. Place the shunt on the line side of the relay but after the fuse; putting it after the relay means the shunt is live even when the load is off. Never rely on the PCB copper alone for the high-current path—use thick traces or a bus bar.

6. The dominant failure that can make the USB live is dielectric breakdown or surface flashover in the digital isolator or across the 8 mm gap during a mains surge or insulation failure. Design it out by using only a reinforced-rated isolator, adding 8 mm slots or barriers on the PCB, fitting a proper MOV and gas-discharge tube on the mains input, and ensuring the fuse clears before any wiring or trace melts. In Option B the same risk exists if the CT or VT insulation punctures; that is why they cannot be trusted as the barrier.

7. You have not addressed what happens to the SELV side when the Mean Well IRM fails or when its output capacitor shorts. A single-point failure there can put raw mains-referenced voltage on the ESP32, USB, and everything else even if the metering isolation is perfect. Add a secondary-side fuse or polyfuse and a TVS clamp right at the IRM output.
